Meet the Wētā 🦗

Wellington tree wētā, Hemideina crassidens — a large, flightless, nocturnal relative of crickets, found only in New Zealand.

  • Lives in galleries chewed into dead wood
  • A male defends a gallery holding several females
  • Big enough to sit in your palm, and it will bite

313 adults were collected at night on Te Pākeka / Maud Island in March and April 2016, and 11 body parts were measured on each one.

Note

📖 Why wētā

New Zealand has almost no native land mammals. Insects took the roles mammals take elsewhere — and got big doing it.

Wētā are what happens to a cricket when nothing is hunting it from the ground.

Three Kinds of Adult Male

Wētā males stop growing at the 8th, 9th or 10th instar — three discrete adult forms, not a smooth gradient:

  • eighth — small head, small jaws. Sneaks into another male’s gallery
  • ninth — intermediate
  • tenth — enormous head and mandibles. Fights for galleries and holds harems

Three strategies for the same job, living side by side in the same forest.

Tip

🧪 One factor, three levels

  • Response: a body measurement — a number
  • Factor: morph — three groups

Exactly last class’s design. The animals are stranger; the test is identical.

Trap 1: The Females Would Swamp It

There are four groups in that file — and 118 of the 313 animals are female.

Females are much smaller than every male morph. Leave them in and the ANOVA will come back wildly significant, driven almost entirely by sex, and the three male morphs get buried.

Our question is “do the three male morphs differ?” — so the data has to be the three male morphs.

Warning

⚠️ The data must match the question

filter() is not housekeeping. It is where you decide what your test is about.

We will bring the females back in Chunk 4, on purpose, and ask a different question.

Trap 2: head_length Is Circular

The three morphs were defined using head length. From the data’s own documentation:

  • eighth — male, head_length < 18.51
  • ninth — male, 18.51 to 24.15
  • tenth — male, head_length > 24.15

So “does head length differ among morphs?” is guaranteed. The answer was built into the labels.

You would get an enormous F and learn nothing.

Important

⚠️ Circular reasoning, in code

If the grouping variable was made from the response variable, the test is meaningless.

Nothing in R will stop you. Anova() will hand you p < 2e-16 and a gold star.

You have to catch this.

So: Males Only, Foreleg Length

weta_df <- weta_raw %>%
  filter(sex == "m", !is.na(foretibia)) %>%
  mutate(morph = factor(morph,
                        levels = c("eighth", "ninth", "tenth")))

weta_df %>% count(morph)
# A tibble: 3 × 2
  morph      n
  <fct>  <int>
1 eighth    78
2 ninth     79
3 tenth     38

Note

🧠 levels with no labels this time

The groups are already words, so factor() only needs levels — to fix the order.

Left alone R sorts alphabetically: eighth, ninth, tenth. That happens to be right. Do not rely on luck — say it out loud in the code.

🧩 Chunk 2 of 4 · The Ears

We will cover: how a wētā hears, and a result that looks like a discovery.

Wētā Hear Through Their Knees

A wētā has no ears on its head. It hears through a tympanic membrane on the foreleg tibia — a drum on the knee.

Our file has that membrane’s area, in mm², as ear.

So we can ask a real behavioural question:

The sneaks have to listen for the fighters. Do the three morphs differ in ear size?

Tip

🧪 Why this is a good question

The three morphs play different games.

A sneak that hears a resident male coming survives. A fighter does not need to sneak.

If hearing mattered differently to them, you might see it in the hardware.

Stop

Before you write that sentence, look back at the table from Chunk 1.

Mean pronotum — body size — by morph:

  • eighth 6.37 mm
  • ninth 6.93 mm
  • tenth 7.52 mm

The tenth-instar males have bigger ears. They also have bigger everything. They are bigger animals.

Warning

⚠️ What did we actually test?

We asked: do the morphs differ in ear size? → Yes.

We wanted to ask: do the morphs differ in hearing?

Those are not the same question, and Anova() cannot tell them apart.

🧩 Chunk 3 of 4 · The Correction

We will cover: one mutate(), a units problem, and a result that disappears.

Measure the Ear Relative to the Animal

The fix is to divide ear size by body size — but there is a units problem first.

  • ear is an area, in mm²
  • pronotum is a length, in mm

Dividing an area by a length is meaningless. So first take the square root of the area, which turns it back into a length, and then divide:

ear_rel = sqrt(ear) / pronotum

Now it is a length over a length — a ratio, with no units at all.

Note

📐 Check your units

This is the oldest trick in quantitative biology and it catches errors nothing else catches.

If the units of your answer are nonsense, the answer is nonsense — however good the p-value looks.

One mutate()

weta_rel <- weta_df %>%
  mutate(ear_rel = sqrt(ear) / pronotum)

weta_rel %>%
  group_by(morph) %>%
  summarise(n = n(),
            mean_ear      = round(mean(ear), 2),
            mean_ear_rel  = round(mean(ear_rel), 4))
# A tibble: 3 × 4
  morph      n mean_ear mean_ear_rel
  <fct>  <int>    <dbl>        <dbl>
1 eighth    78     2.5         0.248
2 ninth     79     2.98        0.249
3 tenth     38     3.42        0.246

Look at Those Two Columns

Raw ear area: 2.50 → 2.99 → 3.42. Climbing hard.

Relative ear: 0.2485 → 0.2490 → 0.2459. Flat.

The third column is the same three groups, the same ear measurements, the same animals — expressed as a fraction of body size.

And there is nothing there.

Tip

🎯 Predict it now

The means are flat to three decimal places.

What is F going to be? What will the letters look like?

What Changed, and What Did Not

Both results are correct. They answer different questions:

  • Raw ear answers: “is a tenth-instar male’s hearing organ physically bigger?” → Yes, clearly.
  • Relative ear answers: “has the hearing organ been scaled up more than the rest of the animal?” → No.

A tenth-instar male has a bigger ear for exactly the reason it has a bigger leg: it is a bigger wētā.

Warning

⚠️ Which would you report?

For a paper about hearing, the relative one — the raw result would overstate the finding badly.

For a paper about how big the organ is, the raw one.

Neither is a trick. You have to say which question you asked.

How to Report a Non-Significant ANOVA

Important

📝 Say it with the same confidence

Relative tympanic membrane size did not differ detectably among the three male morphs (one-way ANOVA, F(2, 192) = 0.80, p = 0.449, η² = 0.008), with homogeneous variances (Levene’s test p = 0.59) and normally distributed residuals (Shapiro–Wilk p = 0.50). Raw membrane area did differ (F(2, 192) = 122.2, p < 0.001), but so did body size, and the difference did not survive scaling.

Three Rules for a Null Result

  • Report the numbers anyway. F, both df, p, and η². “Not significant” on its own is not a result
  • Say it with the assumption checks. A null from a model that passes its checks means something. A null from a broken model means nothing
  • Never write “no difference.” Write “no detectable difference”, or “we did not detect a difference”

Warning

⚠️ Absence of evidence

Remember cld()’s own note from last class:

we cannot show them to be different — but we also did not show them to be the same

With n = 38 in the smallest group, a small real difference would have slipped past us. Saying so is part of the result.

🧩 Chunk 4 of 4 · Bring the Females Back

We will cover: four groups instead of three, and a ladder that stops short.

A Different Question Needs Different Data

In Chunk 1 we dropped the females because they would swamp a question about male morphs.

Now we ask a question they belong in:

Across all four adult forms, is the foreleg scaled up differently?

The foreleg is the weapon — a tenth-instar male fights with it. If it has been built up beyond general body size, relative foreleg length should separate the groups.

Tip

🎯 Same correction, new trait

fore_rel = foretibia / pronotum

Both are lengths in mm, so no square root this time. Just divide.

Females, then eighth, then ninth — all separate. But ninth and tenth share c: 5 of the 6 pairs are significant, and that one is not.

Relative foreleg length rises steeply, then plateaus.

What That Plateau Means

This result survived the body-size correction — unlike the ears. The foreleg really has been built up more than the animal as a whole.

But it stops. Going from ninth to tenth instar makes a wētā bigger without making its foreleg relatively longer again.

Whatever the tenth-instar morph is doing differently, by that point it is doing it with overall size, not with leg proportion.

Warning

⚠️ And the honest caveat

n = 38 for the tenth morph.

ninth and tenth sharing a letter is a failure to detect a difference. With 380 tenth-instar males it might well split.

Say that, then stop.

Recap

  • The data must match the question — filter() decides what your test is about
  • A circular response is meaningless — head_length defined the morphs, so never test it
  • Raw size differences mostly measure body size. Divide by a body-size measure and ask again
  • Check your units — ear is an area, so sqrt() before dividing by a length
  • Ears: p < 0.001 → p = 0.449. Same data, one mutate(), opposite conclusion
  • Forelegs: the difference survived the correction — then plateaued between ninth and tenth
  • Report nulls fully — F, df, p, η², assumption checks, and “no detectable difference”
