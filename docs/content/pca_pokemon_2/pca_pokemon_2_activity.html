Today the whole job is interpretation — looking at numbers and deciding what they mean. There is no output that will tell you whether you got it right. That is not a flaw in the method; it is what the method is for.

Part 1 · Where we left off, and starting again

Last class: six correlated stats, impossible to plot. PCA found new axes by blending them. PC1 = 39.6%, PC2 = 21.5%, together 61.1%. PC1 had all-positive loadings, so we called it overall power, and checked it — Magikarp at the bottom, Mewtwo at the top.

Three things were missing: what PC2 means, a picture, and a way to see the original stats on that picture.

▶ Run this:

library(tidyverse)
library(ggrepel)

pokemon <- read_csv("data/pokemon_151.csv")

poke_stats <- pokemon %>%
  select(hp, attack, defense, sp_attack, sp_defense, speed)

poke_pca <- prcomp(poke_stats, center = TRUE, scale. = TRUE)

summary(poke_pca)

Part 2 · The loadings table

▶ Run this:

round(poke_pca$rotation[, 1:3], 2)

One row per original stat, one column per PC. A loading is how much that stat contributes to that axis:

  • big number → matters a lot to that axis
  • near zero → barely involved
  • the sign → which direction

PC1 — all the same sign

Stat PC1 loading
sp_defense 0.54
sp_attack 0.45
attack 0.42
hp 0.38
speed 0.32
defense 0.28

Every one positive, none of them tiny. To score high on PC1 you must be high on everything.

Important✅ The rule for all-positive loadings

An axis where every loading shares a sign is a “total” axis — overall size, overall strength, overall abundance.

You will see this constantly. PC1 is very often a total, because the single biggest way things differ is usually just how much of them there is.

Part 3 · PC2 has mixed signs

Stat PC2 loading
defense +0.59 ⬆️
attack +0.43 ⬆️
hp +0.19
sp_defense −0.17
sp_attack −0.33 ⬇️
speed −0.54 ⬇️

Two big positives and two big negatives on the same axis.

Important✅ The rule for mixed signs

Mixed signs mean a trade-off, not a total.

PC2 is not measuring how much. It is measuring which kind — being strong in one way instead of another.

  • High PC2 = defense + attack
  • Low PC2 = speed + sp_attack

Naming it

High PC2 means tough and hard-hitting but slow. Low PC2 means fast and special-attacking but fragile. Anyone who has played the game already has names for these:

  • High PC2 → a tank
  • Low PC2 → a glass cannon

PC2 is a “tank ↔︎ glass cannon” axis, and it carries 21.5% of all the variation in six columns.

Warning⚠️ You named it. The maths did not.

prcomp() returned a column of numbers labelled PC2. It has no idea what a tank is.

Naming an axis is interpretation, and it is your responsibility. It is also the single most valuable thing you do in a PCA — and the easiest place to fool yourself.

✏️ Q1: hp loads +0.19 on PC2 — small but positive. Does that fit the tank story? Would you mention hp if you were describing this axis in one sentence?

Part 4 · Prove the name from the data

▶ Run this:

poke_scores <- pokemon %>%
  mutate(PC1 = poke_pca$x[, 1],
         PC2 = poke_pca$x[, 2])

poke_scores %>%
  arrange(desc(PC2)) %>%
  select(name, defense, speed, PC2) %>%
  head(4)

poke_scores %>%
  arrange(PC2) %>%
  select(name, defense, speed, PC2) %>%
  head(4)
End of PC2 Who is there Defence Speed
Top Rhydon, Golem, Cloyster, Rhyhorn 95 – 180 25 – 70
Bottom Abra, Kadabra, Alakazam, Jynx 15 – 45 90 – 120

Alakazam is the fastest, most fragile special attacker in the game. Golem is a literal boulder. They sit at opposite ends of an axis built from nothing but six columns of numbers.

Important✅ The two-step, every time
  1. Read the loadings → form a guess about what the axis means
  2. Look at the extremes → check the guess against things you already know

Do them in that order. If step 2 disagrees with step 1, believe step 2.

Part 5 · Finally, the picture

▶ Run this:

ggplot(poke_scores, aes(x = PC1, y = PC2)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.7)

Every dot is one Pokémon. Neither axis is a thing anyone measured — both were invented by prcomp().

  • Left ↔︎ right = weak ↔︎ strong (PC1, 39.6%)
  • Bottom ↔︎ top = glass cannon ↔︎ tank (PC2, 21.5%)

Two dots close together = two Pokémon with similar stat profiles overall. That is the question we could not answer with 15 pairwise scatterplots. And this one flat picture holds 61% of six columns.

Note🧠 The origin is average

The scores were centred, so (0, 0) is the average Pokémon — not zero power.

A dot at the far left is not “no stats”. It is well below average. Scores are in standard deviations, which is why they run roughly −3.5 to +3.5.

Part 6 · Colour by something you know

▶ Run this:

ggplot(poke_scores, aes(x = PC1, y = PC2, color = is_legendary)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.7, size = 2)

The four legendaries sit in a cluster at the far right — high PC1 — and slightly low on PC2. So the game’s strongest Pokémon are not just powerful; they lean a little towards the fast, special-attacking side rather than the tank side.

Important✅ is_legendary was never in the PCA

Look back at Part 1: poke_stats has six columns, and is_legendary is not one of them. prcomp() never saw it. We only used it to colour the finished result.

That is the right way round. Build the axes from the measurements only, then colour by the thing you care about and see whether it lines up.

Put the grouping variable into the PCA and you have built the answer you wanted — the same circular trap as the wētā’s head_length.

Part 7 · Label only the interesting ones

Labelling all 151 gives you a solid black rectangle. So label only the extremes — the ones furthest from the centre, which are the ones a reader will ask about.

  • abs(PC1) is distance from the centre, ignoring direction
  • quantile(abs(PC1), 0.97) is the value only 3% of Pokémon exceed
  • Together: “far from average on PC1, either way”

▶ Run this:

extremes <- poke_scores %>%
  filter(abs(PC1) > quantile(abs(PC1), 0.97) |
         abs(PC2) > quantile(abs(PC2), 0.97))

nrow(extremes)

extremes %>%
  select(name, PC1, PC2)

ggplot(poke_scores, aes(x = PC1, y = PC2)) +
  geom_point(color = "grey70") +
  geom_point(data = extremes, color = "firebrick") +
  geom_text_repel(data = extremes, aes(label = name), size = 3)

The | in the filter means OR, so a Pokémon qualifies by being extreme on either axis, not both.

Three layers, and the order matters — later layers draw on top:

  1. all 151 points, grey, in the background
  2. the extremes, on top, in red
  3. names, only for the extremes

geom_text_repel() is ggrepel’s version of geom_text(): it nudges labels apart so they do not overlap, and draws a little line back to the dot.

Tip🎯 data = inside a layer

Each geom_ can take its own data =. Give it one and that layer ignores the data in ggplot().

That is how you draw two different subsets on one plot — and it works for every geom, not just these.

✏️ Q4: Change 0.97 to 0.90 and re-run, then to 0.99. There is no correct value. How did you decide which looked best, and what does that tell you about the choice?

Part 8 · The biplot

Our plot shows where Pokémon sit. It does not show which direction each original stat points.

A biplot draws both at once:

  • Points = the Pokémon (the scores)
  • Arrows = the original stats (the loadings)

R has a built-in biplot(poke_pca), but it is ugly and hard to control. Building ours from the loadings means you know exactly what the arrows are.

▶ Run this:

arrows_df <- as_tibble(poke_pca$rotation[, 1:2], rownames = "stat") %>%
  mutate(PC1 = PC1 * 4,
         PC2 = PC2 * 4)

arrows_df

ggplot(poke_scores, aes(x = PC1, y = PC2)) +
  geom_point(color = "grey75", alpha = 0.7) +
  geom_segment(data = arrows_df,
               aes(x = 0, y = 0, xend = PC1, yend = PC2),
               arrow = arrow(length = unit(0.2, "cm"))) +
  geom_text_repel(data = arrows_df, aes(label = stat),
                  fontface = "bold", size = 3.4)
Warning⚠️ Why * 4

Loadings run about −0.6 to +0.6. The scores run about −3.5 to +3.5. Draw the arrows at their true length and they vanish into a dot at the centre.

Multiplying by 4 is purely cosmetic. It changes the arrows’ lengths on screen, never their directions — and the directions are the part you interpret. Try 2, or 6, and see.

Reading the arrows

  • Every arrow points right — all six stats increase with PC1; the all-positive loadings, now visible
  • defense points up-right, speed points down-right — nearly opposite vertically, which is the trade-off
  • attack sits with defense; sp_attack and sp_defense lean with speed
  • defense has the longest arrow, hp the shortest — so hp is the stat least involved in these two axes
Important✅ Read angles, not positions
Arrows Meaning
close together correlated stats
at right angles unrelated
opposite trade-off

Part 9 · A trap worth naming

Last class the correlation between speed and defense was −0.05 — essentially unrelated.

But on this biplot their arrows point in nearly opposite directions, which looks like a strong trade-off.

Both are true. The biplot is a shadow. Two arrows can look opposed in the shadow while being unrelated in the full six dimensions.

Warning⚠️ So always say how much you are showing

“PC1 and PC2, which together explain 61.1% of the variance”

Without that number, a reader cannot tell whether your biplot is the whole story or three-fifths of it.

Never show a biplot without its percentages.

Part 10 · Writing up a PCA

There is no p-value, so the structure is different from everything else this term.

Important📝 The model paragraph

Six battle statistics for the 151 original Pokémon were standardised and reduced by principal component analysis. The first two components explained 61.1% of the total variance (PC1 39.6%, PC2 21.5%). PC1 loaded positively on all six statistics (0.28 to 0.54) and is interpreted as overall power: the four legendary Pokémon averaged PC1 = 3.00 against −0.08 for all others, and Magikarp held the lowest score. PC2 contrasted defense (+0.59) and attack (+0.43) against speed (−0.54) and sp_attack (−0.33), separating durable, slow-moving species such as Golem and Cloyster from fast, fragile ones such as Alakazam.

What that paragraph does:

  • Says what went in — six stats, 151 Pokémon
  • Says it was standardised (scale. = TRUE)
  • Gives variance explained, per axis and cumulative
  • Names each axis, and gives the loadings that justify the name
  • Gives evidence from the data — real Pokémon at each end
  • Never says “significant”, because nothing was tested
Warning⚠️ The two failure modes

Too little: “We ran a PCA and PC1 explained 39.6%.” Explained of what? Meaning what?

Too much: “PC1 proves power is one-dimensional.” PCA proves nothing. It describes.

Describe, name, justify. Stop there.
