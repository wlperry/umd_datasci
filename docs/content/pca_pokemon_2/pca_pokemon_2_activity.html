Part 1 · Where we left off, and starting again

Last class: six correlated stats on 1,025 Pokémon, impossible to plot. PCA found new axes along the directions of most spread. PC1 = 44.1%, PC2 = 18.4%, together 62.5%. PC1 had all-positive loadings, so we called it overall power — and checked it against Magikarp and the legendaries.

▶ Already in your skeleton (section 1) — just run it:

library(tidyverse)
library(plotly)
library(ggrepel)

pokemon <- read_csv("data/pokemon_1025.csv") %>%
  mutate(status = case_when(
    is_legendary ~ "legendary",
    is_mythical  ~ "mythical",
    TRUE         ~ "regular"))

poke_stats <- pokemon %>%
  select(hp, attack, defense, sp_attack, sp_defense, speed)

poke_pca <- prcomp(poke_stats, center = TRUE, scale. = TRUE)

summary(poke_pca)

This is last class’s code, so it is written for you. Nothing today depends on objects left over from last time — restart R, run from the top, get the same answer.

Part 2 · The loadings table

▶ Type this (section 2):

round(poke_pca$rotation[, 1:3], 2)

One row per original stat, one column per PC. A loading is how much that stat contributes to that axis: a big number matters a lot, near zero is barely involved, and the sign gives the direction.

PC1 — all the same sign

Stat PC1 loading
sp_defense 0.44
attack 0.43
sp_attack 0.43
hp 0.42
defense 0.39
speed 0.31

Every one positive, all about the same size. To score high on PC1 you must be high on everything.

Important✅ The rule for all-positive loadings

An axis where every loading shares a sign is a “total” axis — overall size, overall strength, overall abundance. PC1 is very often a total, because the biggest way things differ is usually just how much of them there is.

Part 3 · PC2 has mixed signs

No new code — read the PC2 column of the table you just printed.

Stat PC2 loading
defense +0.58 ⬆️
sp_defense +0.16
hp +0.10
attack +0.07
sp_attack −0.36 ⬇️
speed −0.70 ⬇️
Important✅ The rule for mixed signs

Mixed signs mean a trade-off, not a total. PC2 does not measure how much; it measures which kind — strong one way instead of another.

  • High PC2 = high defense
  • Low PC2 = high speed and sp_attack

Naming it

High PC2 is tough but slow. Low PC2 is fast and special-attacking but fragile. Players already have names for these: a tank and a glass cannon. So PC2 is a “tank ↔︎ glass cannon” axis, carrying 18.4% of all the variation.

Warning⚠️ You named it. The maths did not.

prcomp() returned a column labelled PC2. It has no idea what a tank is. Naming an axis is interpretation, and it is your job — and the easiest place to fool yourself. So next, check it.

💭 Think: hp and attack are near zero on PC2. What does that say about them — are they part of the trade-off, or not?

Part 4 · Prove the name from the data

▶ Type this (section 4):

poke_scores <- pokemon %>%
  mutate(PC1 = poke_pca$x[, 1],
         PC2 = poke_pca$x[, 2])

poke_scores %>%
  arrange(desc(PC2)) %>%
  select(name, defense, speed, PC2) %>%
  head(4)

poke_scores %>%
  arrange(PC2) %>%
  select(name, defense, speed, PC2) %>%
  head(4)
End of PC2 Who is there Defence Speed
Top Shuckle, Stakataka, Steelix, Avalugg 184 – 230 5 – 30
Bottom Regieleki, Pheromosa, Deoxys, Accelgor 37 – 50 145 – 200

Shuckle is a turtle that lives inside a rock: defence 230, speed 5. Regieleki is living electricity: speed 200. They sit at opposite ends of an axis built from nothing but six columns of numbers.

Important✅ The two-step, every time
  1. Read the loadings → form a guess about what the axis means
  2. Look at the extremes → check the guess against things you already know

If step 2 disagrees with step 1, believe step 2.

Part 5 · The picture, coloured by status

▶ Type this (section 5):

ggplot(poke_scores, aes(x = PC1, y = PC2, color = status)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.6)
  • Every dot is one Pokémon. Left ↔︎ right is weak ↔︎ strong (PC1, 44.1%). Bottom ↔︎ top is glass cannon ↔︎ tank (PC2, 18.4%)
  • (0, 0) is the average Pokémon, not zero power — the scores were centred
  • The legendaries and mythicals sit on the far right, and a little below the middle on PC2. The strongest Pokémon lean slightly towards fast rather than tank
Important✅ status was never in the PCA

Look back at Part 1: poke_stats has six columns, and status is not one of them. prcomp() never saw it; we only used it to colour the finished result.

That is the right way round. Build the axes from the measurements only, then colour by the thing you care about. Put the group into the PCA and you have built the answer you wanted — the same circular trap as the wētā’s head_length.

💭 Think: Suppose you were studying skulls from two species and put “species” into the PCA as a 0/1 column. What would go wrong?

The same plot as last class — now that you know what both axes mean.

  • Hover to find Shuckle alone at the top and Regieleki at the bottom
  • text = name is ignored by ggplot (it may warn — that is expected) and picked up by ggplotly()

💭 Think: Find two dots right next to each other and hover over both. Look up their six stats. Are they similar Pokémon?

Part 7 · Label only the interesting ones

Hovering is for you, exploring. A figure in a report or poster cannot be hovered — it needs names printed on it. But 1,025 labels is a solid black rectangle, so label only the extremes.

  • abs(PC1) is distance from the centre, ignoring direction
  • quantile(abs(PC1), 0.99) is the value only 1% of Pokémon exceed
  • The | means OR — a Pokémon is kept if it is extreme on either axis

▶ Type this (section 7):

extremes <- poke_scores %>%
  filter(abs(PC1) > quantile(abs(PC1), 0.99) |
         abs(PC2) > quantile(abs(PC2), 0.99))

nrow(extremes)

ggplot(poke_scores, aes(x = PC1, y = PC2)) +
  geom_point(color = "grey75") +
  geom_point(data = extremes, color = "firebrick") +
  geom_text_repel(data = extremes, aes(label = name), size = 3)

22 Pokémon make the cut. Three layers, and the order matters — later layers draw on top:

  1. all 1,025 points, grey, in the background
  2. the 22 extremes, on top, in red
  3. names, only for the extremes

geom_text_repel() is ggrepel’s version of geom_text(): it nudges labels apart and draws a little line back to each dot.

Tip🎯 data = inside a layer

Each geom_ can take its own data =. Give it one and that layer ignores the data in ggplot(). That is how you draw two subsets on one plot — and it works for every geom.

💭 Think: Change 0.99 to 0.97 in both places and re-run. There is no correct value — how would you decide?

Part 8 · The biplot

Our plot shows where Pokémon sit. It does not show which way each original stat points. A biplot draws both: points = the Pokémon (scores), arrows = the stats (loadings).

▶ The arrows_df block is already in your skeleton (section 8). Type the ggplot() under it:

arrows_df <- as_tibble(poke_pca$rotation[, 1:2], rownames = "stat") %>%
  mutate(PC1 = PC1 * 5,
         PC2 = PC2 * 5)

arrows_df
ggplot(poke_scores, aes(x = PC1, y = PC2)) +
  geom_point(color = "grey80") +
  geom_segment(data = arrows_df,
               aes(x = 0, y = 0, xend = PC1, yend = PC2),
               arrow = arrow(length = unit(0.2, "cm"))) +
  geom_text_repel(data = arrows_df, aes(label = stat),
                  fontface = "bold", size = 3.4)
  • arrows_df is the loadings table turned into a data frame, one row per stat — each row becomes one arrow from (0, 0)
  • geom_segment() draws a line from (x, y) to (xend, yend); arrow = puts a head on it
Warning⚠️ Why * 5

Loadings run about −0.7 to +0.6; the scores run about −4 to +6. At true length the arrows vanish into a dot. Multiplying by 5 is purely cosmetic — it changes the arrows’ lengths on screen, never their directions, and the directions are what you read.

Reading the arrows

  • Every arrow points right — all six stats increase with PC1 (the all-positive loadings, made visible)
  • defense points up-right, speed points down-right — nearly opposite vertically, which is the trade-off. sp_attack leans with speed
  • hp and attack point almost straight right — they are about power, not the trade-off
  • speed has the longest arrow; hp and attack the shortest
Arrows Meaning
close together correlated stats
at right angles unrelated
opposite trade-off

Part 9 · A trap worth naming

Last class, the correlation between speed and defense was 0.01 — completely unrelated. But on this biplot their arrows point in nearly opposite directions, which looks like a strong trade-off.

Both are true. The biplot is a shadow — two of six dimensions. Two arrows can look opposed in the shadow while being unrelated in the full six.

Warning⚠️ So always say how much you are showing

“PC1 and PC2, which together explain 62.5% of the variance”

Without that number a reader cannot tell whether your biplot is the whole story or two-thirds of it. Never show a biplot without its percentages.

Part 10 · Writing up a PCA

There is no p-value, so the structure is different from everything else this term.

Important📝 The model paragraph

Six battle statistics for all 1,025 Pokémon were standardised and reduced by principal component analysis. The first two components explained 62.5% of the total variance (PC1 44.1%, PC2 18.4%). PC1 loaded positively on all six statistics (0.31 to 0.44) and is interpreted as overall power: the 71 legendary Pokémon averaged PC1 = 2.38 against −0.24 for regular Pokémon, and Magikarp sat among the lowest scores. PC2 contrasted defense (+0.58) against speed (−0.70) and sp_attack (−0.36), separating slow, durable species such as Shuckle and Steelix from fast, fragile ones such as Regieleki and Pheromosa.

What that paragraph does:

  • Says what went in — six stats, 1,025 Pokémon — and that it was standardised
  • Gives variance explained, per axis and cumulative
  • Names each axis, with the loadings that justify the name
  • Gives evidence from the data — real Pokémon at each end
  • Never says “significant”, because nothing was tested
Warning⚠️ The two failure modes

Too little: “We ran a PCA and PC1 explained 44.1%.” Of what? Meaning what?

Too much: “PC1 proves power is one-dimensional.” PCA describes; it proves nothing.
