In-class Activity 15: Pivoting Lake Superior Ice Data

Today’s Objectives

  1. Read a messy, wide government text file directly from the web
  2. Say why wide data breaks ggplot() and group_by()
  3. Use pivot_longer() to make it tidy — and pivot_wider() to make a summary table
  4. Fix a real-world date wrinkle: winters that cross the new year
  5. Rebuild NOAA’s “spaghetti plot” of every ice season
  6. Use slice_max() to pull each winter’s peak ice cover
  7. Fit lm() to ask whether maximum ice cover is declining
  8. Repeat the whole thing on a different Great Lake, for homework

Note🔮 Predict before you run

Pivoting is easy to fake your way through by staring at dim() after the fact. Before each pivot, write down the shape you expect — how many rows, how many columns — then run it. A wrong prediction points straight at the argument you misunderstood: cols, names_to, or values_to.

Keep the plots plain today — no labels, colors, or themes except where the skeleton already has them. The goal is to read them.

TipThe invisible(NULL) lines in the skeleton

Every chunk ends with invisible(NULL). It is a bumper. Press Ctrl/Cmd + Enter on a chunk’s last real line and your cursor jumps to the next line that has code on it — without the bumper, that is the next chunk, way down past the answer box. With it, the cursor stops right there and you keep your place. It does nothing and prints nothing.

🧩 Chunk 1 — Why wide data is a problem (after lecture Chunk 1)

Parts 1–2: read NOAA’s raw file and inspect its wide shape.

Part 1 · Load libraries and read the raw file

This chunk is pre-filled in the skeleton. Run it and read it.

url <- "https://www.glerl.noaa.gov/data/ice/glicd/daily/sup.txt"

ice_raw <- read.table(url, header = TRUE,
                      na.strings = c("-999", "-99.00", "NA")) %>%
  rownames_to_column(var = "date") %>%
  as_tibble()

⚠️ Watch out! This file is space-separated, not comma-separated — that is why it needs read.table() and not read_csv(). The day labels (Nov-10…) arrive as row names, so rownames_to_column() turns them into a real date column.

✏️ Q1: Why can’t read_csv() open this file?

Part 2 · Inspect the wide shape

Write the two lines that report the shape of ice_raw and its column names.

✏️ Q2: Rows, columns, what one row represents (a day? a year? a day-of-winter?), and what the column names look like.

✏️ Q3: R glued an X onto the front of every year column — X1973. Why? (What rule do R column names have to follow?)

✏️ Q4: “Year” is hiding as 50-odd column names, not as values in a column. Explain why that makes ggplot(aes(x = year)) and group_by(year) impossible right now.

🧩 Chunk 2 — Reshape with pivot_longer & fix dates (after lecture Chunk 2)

Parts 3–4: pivot the data long, then fix the winters that cross the new year.

Part 3 · Pivot from wide to long

🔮 Predict first: ice_raw is about 209 rows × 55 columns. After pivot_longer() collapses every year column, how many columns will the result have, and roughly how many rows? Write your guess in the skeleton before you run it.

Type the pivot_longer() call. It needs four arguments: cols, names_to, names_prefix, and values_to. Call the result ice_long, then arrange() it by year and date and check its shape.

✏️ Q5: Rows and columns in ice_long. Was your prediction close?

✏️ Q6: What does names_prefix = "X" do? What would the year column hold if you left it out?

Part 4 · Fix winters that cross the new year

An ice season runs Nov → May, but NOAA labels the whole season by the year it ends in. So Nov-10 under column 1974 really happened in November 1973.

Type the mutate() that turns year numeric, builds calendar_year with if_else(), pastes a real full_date together with ymd(), and pulls month out of it — then drops rows with no ice value or no date. Call the result ice_clean.

⚠️ Watch out! ymd() will warn that about 40 dates “failed to parse”. Those are Feb-29 in years that had no February 29th. drop_na() removes them, which is exactly right — there was no such day.

✏️ Q7: Explain the if_else() in plain English: the condition tested, what happens when it is TRUE, and why we subtract 1 from year.

✏️ Q8: How many rows did drop_na() remove, and what were they? (Compare nrow(ice_long) and nrow(ice_clean).)

🧩 Chunk 3 — Summarize & pivot back wider (after lecture Chunk 3)

Parts 5–6: summarize the tidy data, then rebuild a readable table with pivot_wider().

Part 5 · Summarize now that we’re long

Type the group_by() / summarize() that gives the mean ice cover for every year–month combination. Call it monthly_avg.

✏️ Q9: This is the same pattern you used in Summary Statistics. Why was it impossible to run on ice_raw?

Part 6 · Pivot back to a wide summary table

🔮 Predict first: pivot_wider() will make one row per year and one column per month. Roughly how many rows (years) and columns (months + 1)?

Type the pivot_wider() call on ice_clean, using id_cols, names_from, values_from, and values_fn = mean. arrange() the result by year so it reads top to bottom, and print it.

✏️ Q10: Rows and columns in the table. Was your prediction close? Why is a Jun column there, and why is it almost all NA?

✏️ Q11: When would you reach for pivot_longer(), and when for pivot_wider()? One sentence each.

💡 Key idea: long is for computing — group_by, summarize, ggplot. Wide is for reading — a table a human scans. The same data, shaped for two different audiences.

🧩 Chunk 4 — Visualize & model the trend (after lecture Chunk 4)

Parts 7–9: rebuild NOAA’s spaghetti plot, pull each year’s peak, and model the trend.

Part 7 · Rebuild NOAA’s spaghetti plot

This is the long one, so it is pre-filled in the skeleton — the prep that maps every winter onto one shared axis, and the three-layer plot. Run it and read it; you are not expected to type this from scratch.

The one idea worth carrying away: to overlay 50 winters on one axis, every date is forced onto a single fake year — October onward becomes 1999, January onward becomes 2000 — so the x-axis runs November → June once.

✏️ Q12: Open NOAA’s published plot at https://www.glerl.noaa.gov/data/ice/spaghetti/sup_ice_compare.png. Did you recreate it? What does the red line represent, and why is the current year drawn last?

✏️ Q13: The thin blue lines use group = year inside aes(). Take it out in your head — what would the plot look like instead, and why?

Part 8 · Pull each winter’s peak ice with slice_max()

🔮 Predict first: slice_max() keeps the single highest ice-cover day per year. How many rows will the result have?

Type the group_by() + slice_max() that keeps one row — the highest ice_cover — per year. Call it max_ice_per_year, and use with_ties = FALSE so you get exactly one row even when two days tie.

✏️ Q14: Rows in the result. Find the lowest-peak and the highest-peak winters: which year, and what value, for each?

✏️ Q15: Look at the date of each year’s peak. Early season or late? Does that date move around much from winter to winter?

Part 9 · Model the trend in maximum ice cover

🔮 Predict first: over 50+ years, is maximum ice cover declining, flat, or rising? And given the huge year-to-year scatter, will the slope come out significant at α = 0.05? Commit to both before you run it.

Type the lm() of ice_cover on year using max_ice_per_year, and summary() it. Then plot the yearly peaks with geom_point() and a geom_smooth(method = "lm") trend line.

✏️ Q16: Report the model honestly.

Slope (% per year) =
p-value for the slope =
R-squared =
Significant at alpha = 0.05?  Y / N

✏️ Q17: The slope is significant, and the R² is small — around 0.13. Say what each of those two numbers is telling you, and why they are not in conflict.

✏️ Q18: One sentence: is Lake Superior’s peak ice cover changing, and how confident are you given the scatter?

💡 Key idea: a real trend can be significant and still explain only a slice of the variation. Ice cover swings wildly with ENSO and the polar vortex; the long-term decline is a quiet signal underneath a very loud year-to-year noise. Reporting the slope without the R² — or the R² without the slope — tells only half of that.

Homework — a different Great Lake

Write it at the bottom of the same script. Hand it in with your answers as # comments.

H1 · Read and pivot your lake

Read it, pivot_longer() it, fix the Nov/Dec dates, and drop the NAs.

✏️ H-Q1: Your lake, and the rows × columns before and after the pivot.

H2 · Peak ice per winter

slice_max() one row per year.

✏️ H-Q2: How many winters are in your lake’s record? Lowest-peak and highest-peak winters?

H3 · Model the trend

lm(ice_cover ~ year), then plot it with a trend line.

✏️ H-Q3: Slope (% per year), p, R². Significant at α = 0.05?

✏️ H-Q4: Lake Superior’s slope is about −0.7 %/year. Is your lake losing maximum ice faster or slower than Superior, and by how much?

H4 · Explain it

✏️ H-Q5: Using your slope, say in plain language how fast your lake is losing (or gaining) maximum ice cover, and how much total change that adds up to across the whole record.

✏️ H-Q6: Your lake is shallower than Superior. Shallower water holds less heat. Explain in your own words how depth could make one lake’s ice respond faster to the same warming than another’s — and say whether your slope actually came out the way that reasoning predicts.
