In-class Activity 16: Two Fisher Tables into One

NoteAbout the data (constructed for teaching)

Fishers (Pekania pennanti) — a large forest weasel — have been reintroduced to several US states from Canadian source populations. Monitoring produces exactly the shape of data joins are for: one table of the animals released, and a separate table of every field capture / collar event. The two files here are simulated — patterned on real programs such as the Washington Cascades translocations, but the numbers are invented. Use them to learn joins.

Today’s Objectives

  1. Find the key that links two tables, and clean it before joining
  2. Join with left_join() when the key columns are named differently
  3. Compare inner_join(), left_join(), and full_join()
  4. Use semi_join() and anti_join() to filter instead of add columns
  5. Build one analysis-ready table
  6. Join in a table of your own, for homework
Tip📂 Files for today
  • Skeleton script: 16_joins_skeleton.R → save it as scripts/16_joins.R.
  • Two data files, both in your data/ folder:
    • fisher_individuals.csv — one row per released fisher: sex, age_class, source, site (release site), release_date. Keyed by fisher_id.
    • fisher_captures.csv — one row per capture event: capture_date, site (capture site), mass_kg, collar_id. Keyed by animal_id.
ImportantWhat you hand in
  • You type most of the code. The skeleton pre-fills the libraries and the two read_csv() lines. Every other chunk is a # YOUR CODE: blank — the code is on the lecture slides, so read it there and type it in.
  • You answer the Q boxes as # comments in the script.
  • The homework at the bottom of the skeleton brings in a small table of your own — that part you write yourself.
  • Hand in scripts/16_joins.R. Run it top to bottom (Ctrl/Cmd + Shift + Enter) first — it must run with no errors.
Note🔮 Predict before you run

Before every join, write down the row count you expect. fisher_captures.csv has more rows than fisher_individuals.csv — a fisher can be recaptured many times — and a few capture events are of unmarked animals with no matching individual. A wrong guess almost always means you misjudged the matching, not that R did something strange.

TipThe invisible(NULL) lines in the skeleton

Every chunk ends with invisible(NULL). It is a bumper. Press Ctrl/Cmd + Enter on a chunk’s last real line and your cursor jumps to the next line that has code on it — without the bumper, that is the next chunk, way down past the answer box. With it, the cursor stops right there and you keep your place. It does nothing and prints nothing.

🧩 Chunk 1 — Two tables & a key (after lecture Chunk 1)

Parts 1–3: load both tables and clean the key.

Part 1 · Load both tables

This chunk is pre-filled in the skeleton — the libraries and the two read_csv() calls. Run it, then write the two lines that report each table’s shape.

✏️ Q1: Rows and columns in each table. Which has more rows, and why might that be?

Part 2 · Find the key

Print the first few rows of the identifying columns of each table and compare them.

✏️ Q2: What column links the two tables, and what is it called in each? Are the names the same?

✏️ Q3: Both tables also have a site column. Do the two mean the same thing? Look again at what each table is a row of.

Part 3 · Clean the key

count() the capture key and look at the values. Then write the mutate() that fixes its case, and check how many distinct keys each table has.

✏️ Q4: animal_id is stored as f07 but fisher_id is F07. Why must you fix the case before joining? What would a join have produced if you had not?

✏️ Q5: One value of animal_id is not a fisher ID at all. What is it, and what does it mean about those capture events?

💡 Key idea: a key has to be unique in the lookup table and the same type and format in both. Almost every failed join is one of those two things.

🧩 Chunk 2 — Your first join (after lecture Chunk 2)

Parts 4–5: the left join.

Part 4 · The left join

🔮 Predict first: you want to keep every capture event and attach who the animal is. How many rows will the result have, and roughly how many will have a missing sex?

Type the left_join() of captures_df onto individuals_df. The key columns have different names, so you need by = c("animal_id" = "fisher_id"). Call the result captures_joined, then report its row count and how many rows have an NA sex.

✏️ Q6: Total rows, rows with an identified individual, and rows with NA individual info. Was your prediction close?

Note that the join hands you two site columns — site.x (capture site) and site.y (release site). Real fishers move; those are genuinely different things, and dplyr is right to keep both.

Part 5 · Name the key every time

Redo the Part 4 join and select() down to capture_event, animal_id, capture_date, sex, and age_class so you can read it.

✏️ Q7: Do any of the first rows already show NA for sex? What does that mean about that particular capture event?

🧩 Chunk 3 — The join family (after lecture Chunk 3)

Parts 6–8: inner vs. left vs. full.

Part 6 · Run all three joins

🔮 Predict first: rank these three by row count, most to fewest: inner_join, left_join, full_join.

Store the key once in a variable, then run all three joins and report the three row counts side by side with c().

✏️ Q8: The three row counts. Was your ranking right? Why does full have more rows than left — which fishers does it add back?

Part 7 · What each join dropped or kept

✏️ Q9: In your own words, what does each of inner_join, left_join, and full_join keep?

✏️ Q10: Which capture events does inner_join drop that left_join keeps? Which fishers does full_join add that neither of the others includes?

Part 8 · Pick the right join

✏️ Q11: You want a table of every capture event for a survival analysis, keeping the event even when the animal was unmarked. Which join, and why?

💡 Key idea: decide which rows you cannot afford to lose first, then pick the join. An unmarked-animal capture is still a capture, so an event-level analysis calls for left_join with captures on the left — not inner_join.

🧩 Chunk 4 — Filtering joins & the payoff (after lecture Chunk 4)

Parts 9–11: anti/semi joins and the analysis-ready table.

Part 9 · anti_join() — what’s missing

🔮 Predict first, two ways: (a) how many capture events have no matching individual? (b) how many released fishers were never recaptured?

Write both anti_join()s. Note that (b) runs in the other direction — individuals on the left — and that the by = flips with it.

✏️ Q12: Capture events with no individual, and released fishers never recaptured. Were your predictions close?

✏️ Q13: Did anti_join() add any columns, or only filter rows? Check the column names against the table you started from.

Part 10 · semi_join() — keep only matches

Write the semi_join() that keeps the released fishers that were recaptured at least once, and count them.

✏️ Q14: How is semi_join() different from inner_join()? Look at the columns, not just the rows.

✏️ Q15: recaptured rows + never_seen rows should equal nrow(individuals_df). Does it? What would it mean if it did not?

Part 11 · Build one analysis-ready table

Write the pipe that inner_join()s the two tables, mutate()s a days_since_release column from the two dates, and select()s down to the columns worth keeping — renaming site.x to capture_site and site.y to release_site as you go. Then write_csv() it to data/fisher_joined.csv.

✏️ Q16: Rows in your analysis table. Why is it smaller than captures_df?

✏️ Q17: Why inner_join() here, when Part 8 argued for left_join()? What is different about this table’s job?

✏️ Q18: Check the range of days_since_release. Are all the values positive? What would a negative value have told you about the data?

Homework — summarize it, then join your own table

Write it at the bottom of the same script. Hand it in with your answers as # comments.

H1 · Summarize the joined table

Using fisher_analysis, write two summaries of your own:

  1. Mean mass_kg by sex, with the sample size for each.
  2. Capture-event count by release_site and sex.

✏️ H-Q1: Mean mass of females and of males, with both sample sizes. Fishers are strongly sexually dimorphic — does your answer match that?

✏️ H-Q2: Which release-site-and-sex combination has the most capture events? Does that tell you about the animals, or about where the crews spent their time? Say which, and how you would tell the two apart.

H2 · Recaptures per fisher

Count how many times each identified fisher was caught. Use a filtering join, not a mutating one — you do not need any new columns to do this.

✏️ H-Q3: The most-recaptured fisher and its count, and how many fishers were caught exactly once.

H3 · Join in a table of your own

Build a small table with tibble() — four or five rows is plenty — keyed by fisher_id, holding something you invent: a home-range size in km², a collar battery status, a field crew name. Then join it onto the fisher data on the key.

✏️ H-Q4: Paste your tibble() and say which join you chose and why.

✏️ H-Q5: Your table covers only some of the fishers. How many rows came back with NA from your new column, and is that a problem or exactly what you asked for?

H4 · The thing that surprised you

✏️ H-Q6: Across everything in this activity, what surprised you most about joining these two tables? Be specific — name the numbers or the rows that surprised you, and say what you expected instead. “Nothing surprised me” is not an answer; if the joins went smoothly, the surprise might be how many capture events could not be tied to a known animal, or how many released fishers were never seen again.

Review and checkpoint

At this point you should be able to:

Run your whole script top to bottom with Ctrl/Cmd + Shift + Enter. It must run clean.

💡 Key idea: fisher_individuals.csv and fisher_captures.csv were each incomplete on their own — one had no field history, the other had no animal attributes. fisher_joined.csv is what you get once a shared key ties them together. Your own project data will almost certainly arrive the same way.
