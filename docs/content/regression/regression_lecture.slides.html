What Is Linear Regression?

Linear regression fits the best straight line through a cloud of points so you can predict one number (Y) from another number (X).

Role Variable On the plot
Explanatory (X) what you can measure easily x-axis
Response (Y) what you actually want to know y-axis

X is usually the easy measurement. Y is the expensive one. Regression gives you an equation that converts X into Y.

📖 W&S §17.1 p. 540–541

Regression vs. the t-test:

Test The question
t-test Do two groups have different means?
Regression Does X predict Y?

Use regression when:

  • both variables are numeric
  • X could reasonably predict Y
  • you want an equation, not just a yes/no

A Real Example from the Textbook

W&S Example 17.1 — “the lion’s nose.” 32 male lions of known age.

Aging a wild lion is hard — you have to have watched it since it was a cub.

But a male lion’s nose darkens as it ages, and you can photograph a nose from a truck.

  • X = proportion of the nose that is black — easy
  • Y = age in years — what you want

Fit the line once on lions of known age, and from then on you can estimate a lion’s age from a photograph.

📖 W&S §17.1, Example 17.1

Our Version of the Same Problem

The problem: we want leaf area in cm², but we don’t have a leaf-area meter. We have a balance.

The fix — a calibration curve:

  1. Cut paper squares of known area (1, 4, 16 … 567 cm²)
  2. Weigh each square
  3. Fit the line: area ~ mass
  4. Trace a leaf onto the same paper and weigh the tracing
  5. Put the tracing mass into the equation → predicted leaf area

Why it works: paper from one stock has a constant area per gram, so mass and area move together almost perfectly.

Known area (cm²) Squares weighed
1 25
4 32
16 24
36 3
64 7
100 14
225–567 13

118 paper squares in all.

Remember paper_mass_g in our leaf data? That is the tracing mass. By the end of today it becomes an area.

The Regression Equation

\[\hat{Y} = a + bX\]

Symbol Name What it means
\(\hat{Y}\) predicted Y what the line says Y should be
a intercept Y when X = 0 — where the line crosses
b slope how much Y changes per 1 unit of X

Ours will read:

\[\widehat{\text{area}} = a + b \times \text{mass}\]

📖 W&S §17.1 pp. 543–545

The slope is the useful one.

b = the extra cm² you get for each extra gram of paper

That is just the area density of the paper stock — and it is the number that does the converting.

The intercept is a bookkeeping number.

a = the predicted area of a piece of paper weighing nothing

Physically that should be 0. A small value near zero is fine; it is measurement noise, not biology.

What Is a Residual?

Red = the residuals for the lion data.

A residual is one point’s miss:

\[\text{residual} = \text{observed} - \text{predicted}\]

  • A point above the line → positive residual (that lion was older than its nose suggested)
  • A point below the line → negative residual
  • A point on the line → residual of 0

The line R gives you is the one that makes those red segments as small as possible overall. That is all “least squares” means — and R does the work.

📖 W&S §17.1 p. 542

R² — How Tight Is the Fit?

R² runs from 0 to 1. It is the fraction of the variation in Y that the line accounts for.

R² Reads as
1.00 points sit on the line
0.62 X explains 62% of the variation
0.00 the line tells you nothing

Both of these are good regressions. Nose blackness really does predict a lion’s age — just with scatter, because lions vary. Paper is manufactured to be uniform, so it has almost none.

In biology, R² of 0.3–0.7 is normal and useful. Don’t expect 0.99.

📖 W&S §17.4 p. 555

R² and the p-value Answer Different Questions

The question it answers
p-value Is there really a relationship, or could this slope be chance?
R² How tightly do the points follow the line?

You can have a significant slope with a low R² — a real relationship with a lot of scatter. That is most of ecology.

Important

The slope is the hypothesis test.

H₀: the slope is 0 — X does not predict Y

Hₐ: the slope is not 0 — X does predict Y

summary() gives you the p-value for exactly that test, and we will find it on the next few slides.

📖 W&S §17.3 pp. 551–553

Assumptions of Regression

Four things need to be roughly true for the p-value and the intervals to mean anything (W&S §17.5):

# Assumption How you check it
1 Linear — Y really does follow a straight line the scatter plot, then plot(model) panel 1
2 Independent — one observation doesn’t depend on another your study design
3 Equal variance — the scatter is about the same all the way along plot(model) panels 1 and 3
4 Normal residuals — the misses are bell-shaped plot(model) panel 2

Important

Check the residuals, not the raw data.

Normality here is about the misses — the red segments — not about Y itself. Y is supposed to go up with X; of course it isn’t bell-shaped.

The good news: once you have a fitted model, plot() draws all of these for you. No extra code.

Same habit as the t-test: check → decide → report.

📖 W&S §17.5 pp. 557–560

Load Libraries and Data

# Load packages at the top ------------------------------
library(readxl)    # read Excel files
library(tidyverse) # dplyr + ggplot2
library(janitor)   # clean_names()
# Load the paper calibration data ----------------------
paper_df <- read_excel("data/paper_area_weights.xlsx")

Two columns, 118 rows: area_cm2 is the known area of each square, mass_g is what it weighed.

Always Plot It First

# Scatter plot of area against mass --------------------
scatter_plot <- paper_df %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = mass_g, y = area_cm2)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method="lm")
scatter_plot

Three plain lines. aes(x = , y = ) puts the explanatory variable on x and the response on y, and geom_point() draws the dots.

What you are looking for:

  • Straight? → a line is the right tool
  • Scatter the same all the way along?
  • Any point way off on its own?

Here: straight, tight, nothing strange. Fit the line.

Note

Never fit a regression you haven’t looked at. A curve, a funnel, or one wild point all change what you should do next.

Fit the Model with lm()

# Fit the regression line ------------------------------
paper_lm_model <- lm(area_cm2 ~ mass_g,
                     data = paper_df)

Nothing prints. lm() hands back a model object that is holding the answer — summary() is what shows it to you.

The formula is the whole trick:

lm(Y ~ X, data = df)
  • Y — the response — goes on the left of ~
  • X — the explanatory variable — goes on the right
  • ~ reads as “is predicted by”

So area_cm2 ~ mass_g is “area is predicted by mass.”

Warning

⚠️ Get the order wrong and R will still answer. lm(mass_g ~ area_cm2) runs fine — it just fits the question you didn’t ask.

There are only four numbers in here that you need, and we will take them one at a time:

  1. the slope (mass_g → Estimate)
  2. the intercept ((Intercept) → Estimate)
  3. the slope’s p-value (mass_g → Pr(>|t|))
  4. the R² (Multiple R-squared)

Reading It — 1. The Coefficients Table

Coefficients:
            Estimate Std. Error t value Pr(>|t|)
(Intercept)   0.2792     0.1812   1.541    0.126
mass_g      130.5234     0.1473 885.964   <2e-16

The Estimate column is the equation.

  • (Intercept) = 0.28 → that is a
  • mass_g = 130.52 → that is b, the slope

So the fitted line is:

\[\widehat{\text{area}} = 0.28 + 130.52 \times \text{mass}\]

Say the slope out loud, with units:

Every extra 1 gram of this paper is another 130.5 cm² of area.

That is the conversion factor we came for.

The other three columns, in one line each:

Column What it is
Std. Error how uncertain that estimate is
t value Estimate ÷ Std. Error
Pr(>|t|) the p-value for that row

📖 W&S §17.3 p. 551

Reading It — 2. The Two p-Values

(Intercept)   0.2792   ...   0.126
mass_g      130.5234   ...  <2e-16

The slope row is the one that matters. Pr(>|t|) for mass_g is <2e-16 — R’s way of writing “smaller than I can print.”

  • H₀: the slope is 0 → mass tells you nothing about area
  • p < 0.001 → reject H₀. Mass predicts area.

The intercept row: p = 0.126, so the intercept is not distinguishable from 0 — exactly what physics says it should be. A piece of paper with no mass has no area.

Tip

Which p-value do I report?

The slope’s. That is the biological question. Nobody reports the intercept’s p-value unless zero means something special.

Note

Never write p = 0. When R prints <2e-16, you write p < 0.001.

Reading It — 3. R² and the Bottom Line

Residual standard error: 1.764 on 116 degrees of freedom
Multiple R-squared:  0.9999, Adjusted R-squared:  0.9999
F-statistic: 7.849e+05 on 1 and 116 DF,  p-value: < 2.2e-16
  • Multiple R-squared = 0.9999 → mass explains 99.99% of the variation in area. That is manufactured paper, not biology.
  • F-statistic with 1 and 116 DF, p < 2.2e-16 → the overall test of the model. With one X it tells you the same thing as the slope’s p-value.
  • Residual standard error = 1.76 cm² → a typical miss is about 1.8 cm².

Where the df come from:

  • 1 = one explanatory variable
  • 116 = 118 squares − 2 numbers we estimated (a and b)

Use Adjusted R-squared once you have more than one X; with a single X, report Multiple R-squared.

Important

R² = 0.9999 does not mean “everything is fine.” It means the points are close to the line. Whether the assumptions hold is a separate question — and we check it two slides from now.

Draw the Line

# Scatter plot with the fitted line --------------------
calibration_plot <- paper_df %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = mass_g, y = area_cm2)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm")
calibration_plot

One extra line. geom_smooth(method = "lm") fits the same regression again, just for drawing, and adds:

  • the fitted line
  • a grey 95% confidence band around it

The band is narrowest in the middle of the data and flares at the ends — the line is pinned down best where you have the most data. Here it is so narrow you can barely see it.

Note

geom_smooth() defaults to a wiggly curve. method = "lm" is what makes it a straight regression line.

The Same Plot, Dressed Up

Check the Residuals — plot(model)

# All four residual plots in one call ------------------
par(mfrow = c(2, 2))
plot(paper_lm_model)

par(mfrow = c(2, 2)) just says “put the next four plots in a 2×2 grid.”

Panel Name Checks
1 Residuals vs Fitted linear? equal scatter?
2 Normal Q-Q residuals bell-shaped?
3 Scale-Location equal scatter again
4 Residuals vs Leverage any one point running the show

Panel 1 is the one to look at first.

  • ✅ Good: a flat, shapeless cloud around 0
  • ❌ Curved: the relationship isn’t straight
  • ❌ Funnel: the scatter grows as Y grows

What Our Residual Plot Is Telling Us

Look at panel 1 again. The points fan out — a 1 cm² square misses by hundredths of a cm², a 567 cm² square misses by several cm².

That is a funnel: assumption 3 (equal variance) does not hold.

# How big are the misses at each square size? ---------
paper_df %>%
  mutate(residual = residuals(paper_lm_model)) %>%
  group_by(area_cm2) %>%
  summarize(biggest_miss = round(max(abs(residual)), 2))

Important

A great R² and a bad residual plot can live together. R² says the points are near the line. The residual plot says the size of the miss depends on how big the square is — which it does, because a balance has percentage error, not fixed error.

So what do we do?

  • For predicting a leaf tracing (0.1–0.4 g) we are down in the tight end of the line, where a typical miss is a fraction of a cm². The calibration is fine for the job.
  • The confidence intervals at the big-square end are the part you shouldn’t trust.
  • The proper fix — fitting on a log scale, or weighting the points — is a graduate topic. Knowing to look, and saying what you saw, is the undergraduate job.

Predict One Tracing

# Predict the area of one leaf tracing ----------------
# The tracing weighed 0.138 g
one_tracing <- tibble(mass_g = 0.138)

predict(paper_lm_model, newdata = one_tracing)
       1 
18.29144

An 0.138 g tracing is about 18.3 cm² of leaf.

predict() takes two things:

  • the model you fitted
  • newdata = — a small data frame of the X values you want predictions for

Warning

⚠️ The column in newdata must have the same name as in the model. The model was built on mass_g, so newdata needs a column called mass_g. Call it mass and R stops with “object ‘mass_g’ not found.”

This beats doing the arithmetic yourself — no copying the slope, nothing to mistype.

Predict with an Interval

# Two tracings, with 95% prediction intervals ---------
two_tracings <- tibble(mass_g = c(0.092, 0.138))

predict(paper_lm_model,
        newdata = two_tracings,
        interval = "prediction")
       fit       lwr      upr
1 12.28736  8.777018 15.79771
2 18.29144 14.781578 21.80131

The 0.138 g tracing is 18.3 cm², somewhere between about 14.8 and 21.8 cm².

interval = "prediction" adds two columns:

  • fit — the prediction
  • lwr and upr — the 95% range

Two kinds of interval:

interval = Range for Width
"confidence" the average area of all tracings this heavy narrower
"prediction" one particular tracing wider

For a single real leaf, use "prediction".

📖 W&S §17.2 pp. 548–550

How to Report a Regression

The template — fill in from your summary() output:

[What predicted what] (linear regression: F(df₁, df₂) = ___, p ___, R² = ___). The fitted equation was Y = ___ + ___ × X.

Ours:

Paper mass was a very strong predictor of paper area (linear regression: F(1, 116) = 784,900, p < 0.001, R² = 0.9999). The calibration equation was area = 0.28 + 130.52 × mass. Residual scatter increased with square size, so intervals for the largest squares should be treated with caution.

Where every number comes from:

In the sentence In summary()
F and both df the F-statistic: line
p that same line
R² Multiple R-squared
intercept (Intercept) → Estimate
slope mass_g → Estimate

Always include: the test name, F with both df, p, R², and the equation. Add a line about the assumptions you checked.
