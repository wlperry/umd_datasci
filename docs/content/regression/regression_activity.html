In-class Activity 11: Linear Regression

Today’s Objectives

  1. Fit a linear regression with lm(Y ~ X, data)
  2. Read the slope, intercept, p-value, and R² off the summary() output
  3. Draw the fitted line with geom_smooth(method = "lm")
  4. Check the residuals with plot(model)
  5. Use predict() to turn a tracing mass into a leaf area — one value, then a whole column
  6. Write a standard regression results sentence

🧩 Chunk 2 — Fit it and read it (after lecture Chunk 2)

Parts 1–4: load the paper data, plot it, fit the line, read the output.

Part 1 · Load libraries and data

▶ Run this (already at the top of your skeleton):

# Load libraries ---------------------------------------
library(readxl)      # read Excel files
library(tidyverse)   # dplyr + ggplot2
library(janitor)     # clean_names()

# Load the paper calibration data ----------------------
paper_df <- read_excel("data/paper_area_weights.xlsx")

glimpse(paper_df)

118 paper squares. area_cm2 is the known area of each square; mass_g is what it weighed.

✏️ Q1: Which column is the explanatory variable (X) and which is the response (Y)? Why that direction, given that we want leaf areas?

Part 2 · Plot it before you fit it

▶ Run this:

# Scatter plot of area against mass --------------------
paper_df %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = mass_g, y = area_cm2)) +
  geom_point()

Look for three things: is it straight, is the scatter the same all the way along, and is any point off on its own?

✏️ Q2: Straight or curved? Any obvious outlier? Is a straight line the right tool here?

Part 3 · Fit the regression

▶ Run this:

# Fit the regression line ------------------------------
paper_lm_model <- lm(area_cm2 ~ mass_g, data = paper_df)

Nothing prints — lm() stores the answer in a model object. The formula reads Y ~ X, “area is predicted by mass.”

▶ Run this:

# Read the model ---------------------------------------
summary(paper_lm_model)

⚠️ Watch out! Flip the formula to lm(mass_g ~ area_cm2) and R runs happily — it just answers a question you didn’t ask. The response always goes on the left of ~.

Part 4 · Read the summary() output

Everything you need is in four places. Find each one in your output.

What you want Where it is
slope (b) Coefficients table, mass_g row, Estimate column
intercept (a) Coefficients table, (Intercept) row, Estimate column
p-value for the slope Coefficients table, mass_g row, Pr(>|t|) column
R² Multiple R-squared near the bottom
F and df the F-statistic: line at the very bottom

✏️ Q4a: Write down the slope and say what it means with units — finish this sentence: “every extra 1 gram of this paper is another ___ cm² of area.”

✏️ Q4b: Write down the intercept. Its p-value is 0.126 — not significant. Why is that the right answer physically? (What is the area of a piece of paper that weighs nothing?)

✏️ Q4c: Write the fitted equation: area = ____ + ____ × mass

✏️ Q4d: Write down the slope’s p-value and R². What does each one tell you — and why are they answering different questions?

💡 Key idea: R² says how tight the fit is. The p-value says whether the slope is real. A relationship can be real and still scattered — that is most of biology.

🧩 Chunk 3 — Plot it, check it, use it (after lecture Chunk 3)

Parts 5–9: draw the line, check the residuals, predict, report.

Part 5 · Draw the fitted line

▶ Run this:

# Scatter plot with the fitted line --------------------
paper_df %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = mass_g, y = area_cm2)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm")

geom_smooth(method = "lm") adds the regression line plus a grey 95% confidence band. Without method = "lm" you get a wiggly curve instead of a straight line.

✏️ Q5: Where is the grey band widest, and why? (Think about where the data are thickest.)

Part 6 · Check the residuals

A residual is one point’s miss: observed − predicted. plot() on a fitted model draws four residual plots at once — no extra code needed.

▶ Run this:

# All four residual plots in one call ------------------
par(mfrow = c(2, 2))
plot(paper_lm_model)
Panel Name What it checks
1 Residuals vs Fitted straight line? equal scatter?
2 Normal Q-Q are the misses bell-shaped?
3 Scale-Location equal scatter, another view
4 Residuals vs Leverage is one point running the show?

Panel 1 first. A flat shapeless cloud is good. A curve means the relationship isn’t straight. A funnel means the scatter grows as Y grows.

✏️ Q6a: Describe panel 1. Flat cloud, curve, or funnel?

▶ Run this to see the same thing as numbers:

# How big are the misses at each square size? ---------
paper_df %>%
  mutate(residual = residuals(paper_lm_model)) %>%
  group_by(area_cm2) %>%
  summarize(biggest_miss = round(max(abs(residual)), 2))

✏️ Q6b: How big is the worst miss for a 1 cm² square? For a 567 cm² square? Which assumption does that pattern break?

✏️ Q6c: R² is 0.9999 — the best fit you will ever see — and the residual plot still has a problem. In your own words, how can both be true at once?

💡 Key idea: Our leaf tracings weigh 0.1–0.4 g, down at the tight end of the line where a typical miss is a fraction of a cm². The calibration is fine for the job we are using it for. Fixing the funnel properly is a graduate topic — noticing it and saying so is the undergraduate job.

Part 7 · Predict one tracing

▶ Run this:

# Predict the area of one leaf tracing ----------------
one_tracing <- tibble(mass_g = 0.138)

predict(paper_lm_model, newdata = one_tracing)

▶ Run this to add a 95% interval:

# Two tracings, with 95% prediction intervals ---------
two_tracings <- tibble(mass_g = c(0.092, 0.138))

predict(paper_lm_model,
        newdata = two_tracings,
        interval = "prediction")
  • fit is the prediction; lwr and upr are the 95% range
  • interval = "confidence" would give the range for the average tracing of that mass — narrower. For one real leaf, use "prediction".

⚠️ Watch out! newdata must use the same column name as the model. The model was built on mass_g, so the column must be called mass_g. Call it mass and R stops with “object ‘mass_g’ not found.”

✏️ Q7: Record the predicted area for the 0.138 g tracing and its 95% prediction interval. Which tracing is predicted to be larger, and does that make sense?

Part 8 · Convert a whole column of tracings

paper_mass_g in the leaf data is the mass of each leaf’s paper tracing. Now we turn all of them into areas at once.

▶ Run this:

# Load our leaf data ----------------------------------
leaf_df <-
  read_excel("data/2026_09_03_data_sci_leaf_area.xlsx") %>%
  clean_names()

# Turn every tracing mass into an area ----------------
leaf_df <- leaf_df %>%
  mutate(leaf_area_cm2 =
           predict(paper_lm_model,
                   newdata = tibble(mass_g = paper_mass_g)))

leaf_df %>%
  select(teams, shade, mass_g, paper_mass_g, leaf_area_cm2)

Same rule as Part 7: predict() wants a column called mass_g, so we hand it tibble(mass_g = paper_mass_g) — our tracing masses, under the name the model knows.

▶ Run this:

# Predicted leaf area by side -------------------------
leaf_df %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = shade, y = leaf_area_cm2)) +
  geom_boxplot() +
  geom_jitter(width = 0.15)
# Mean predicted area per side ------------------------
leaf_df %>%
  group_by(shade) %>%
  summarize(
    n = sum(!is.na(leaf_area_cm2)),
    mean_area = mean(leaf_area_cm2, na.rm = TRUE)
  )

✏️ Q8a: Record the mean predicted area for each side. Does that match what the t-test told you about leaf mass back in Activities 9–10?

✏️ Q8b: Some leaves have NA for leaf_area_cm2. Why? (Look at paper_mass_g for that team.) Is R right to leave them missing?

✏️ Q8c: Why is “shady leaves are about 1 cm² bigger” a more useful sentence for a reader than “shady leaves weigh 0.02 g more”?

Part 9 · Write the results sentence

Fill in the blanks from your summary() output — do not retype numbers from these instructions, read them off your own screen.

____ was a strong predictor of ____ (linear regression:
F(___, ___) = ____, p ____, R2 = ____). The fitted
equation was area = ____ + ____ x mass.

Then add one sentence about the assumptions — what the residual plot showed and what you are being careful about because of it.

✏️ Q9: Write the full results paragraph in your answer box.

Extension — out of class (~20–30 min)

Turn this in with your script, as # comments at the bottom of the file. paper_df is the same for everyone — but the object you calibrate is one only you have.

E1 · Calibrate something only you have

  1. Cut any shape you like out of ordinary printer or notebook paper — trace your hand, your initials in bubble letters, a rough outline of your home state.
  2. Weigh it in grams on a kitchen or postal scale. No scale? Estimate it against a US penny (2.5 g) or a sheet of printer paper (≈4.5 g), and say that’s what you did.
  3. In R, use paper_lm_model and predict(..., interval = "prediction") to estimate its area from its mass.
  4. Measure the real area the low-tech way: trace it onto graph paper and count squares.

Report, as comments: what the shape was, its mass, the predicted area and interval, your counted area, and whether the counted area landed inside the interval.

E2 · Explain your own numbers

  1. Using your R² from Part 4, say in plain language what fraction of the variation in area the line accounts for — and one thing R² does not tell you.
  2. Your prediction interval in E1 is wider than a confidence interval would be at the same mass. Why is predicting one specific object riskier than estimating an average?
  3. Run max(paper_df$mass_g). Suppose you had cut your shape out of cardboard and it weighed 9 g. Why would predicting its area be a different kind of mistake than the prediction you actually made?

📖 Whitlock & Schluter §17.2 p. 550: “Extrapolation is the prediction of Y at values of X beyond the range of X-values in the data. Extrapolation is problematic because there is no way to ensure the relationship between X and Y continues to be linear.”
