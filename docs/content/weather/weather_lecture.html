Download real weather station data from inside R, summarize it to yearly means, and use lm() to estimate a warming rate in degrees per decade — for Duluth, for each season, and for a city you choose.

Where we left off (Lecture 11 — Regression)

  • lm(Y ~ X, data = df) fits a straight line through a cloud of points
  • summary(model) gives you:
    • the slope — how much Y changes per 1 unit of X
    • its p-value — is that slope real, or could it be chance?
    • R² — how tightly the points follow the line
  • geom_smooth(method = "lm") draws the line
  • plot(model) checks the residuals
Note

✅ Where today comes in

  • Last time X was paper mass and the slope converted grams into cm²
  • Today X is time
    • the slope becomes a rate of change — degrees per year
    • and that is exactly what a “warming rate” is

Goals for Today

  • Find a station for a city you choose and run the whole thing again

🧩 Chunk 1 of 3 · The Question

We will cover: where the data comes from, why the slope of a regression on time is a warming rate, and why you have to summarize before you can see it. No code in this chunk.

Where Does This Data Come From?

  • GSOD = Global Surface Summary of the Day, run by NOAA
    • daily weather from thousands of stations worldwide
    • many records reach back to the 1940s
    • free, and no account needed
  • The GSODR package pulls it straight into R
    • you give it a station ID and a range of years
    • it hands back a data frame

Our station to start:

  • Duluth International Airport
  • Station ID 727450-14913
  • Daily records since 1948
  • Why this matters:
    • this is the same messy, real-world data you will meet in your own project
    • the workflow — download → summarize → model — is the one you will reuse all term
  • And it is yours to reuse:
    • every city with a long station record can be run through the exact same code
    • you will pick one later today

The Slope Is the Warming Rate

Last lecture:

\[\widehat{\text{area}} = a + b \times \text{mass}\]

  • b was cm² per gram — a conversion factor

Today:

\[\widehat{\text{temp}} = a + b \times \text{year}\]

  • b is °C per year — a rate of change

Nothing about the code changes. Only what X means.

  • Reading the slope:
    • b positive → warming
    • b negative → cooling
    • b near 0 → no trend
  • Make it human: °C per year is a tiny number
    • multiply it by 10 → °C per decade
    • 0.027 °C/year → about 0.27 °C per decade
  • The p-value still does the same job
    • H₀: the slope is 0 — no trend at all
    • small p → the trend is real, not chance

Why You Must Summarize First

  • A station reports one row per day
    • 78 years × 365 days ≈ 28,000 rows
  • Every one of those days carries the seasonal cycle
    • January is 30 °C colder than July, every single year
    • that swing is enormous compared with a few tenths of a degree of warming
  • So the trend you want is buried in the noise you don’t
Summary level group_by() Rows left
Daily (raw) none ~28,000
Yearly year 78
Important

Summarizing is not throwing data away.

  • It is choosing which question you are answering
  • Want to study a heat wave? Use daily
  • Want to study climate? Use yearly
  • One group_by(year) %>% summarize() — the same two lines from Summary Statistics
  • That is the whole fix

We will cover: downloading the Duluth record, cleaning the names, summarizing to yearly means, and fitting lm(temp ~ year).

Load Libraries and Download the Data

# Load packages at the top -----------------------------
library(tidyverse) # dplyr + ggplot2
library(janitor)   # clean_names()
library(GSODR)     # NOAA weather station data
# Download daily Duluth weather, 1948-2025 -------------
# clean_names() makes every column lower case
duluth_df <- get_GSOD(years = 1948:2025,
                      station = "727450-14913") %>%
  clean_names()
  • get_GSOD() takes years and a station and goes and gets the data
    • 1948:2025 is a vector — “every year in this range”
    • it takes a minute; 78 years of daily data is a lot
  • clean_names() is the important habit here
    • NOAA ships columns as TEMP, YEARMODA, MXSPD
    • R is case-sensitive, so temp would error
    • clean_names() makes everything lower case and snake case, once, at the top
Tip

Clean the names as you load the data, and you never think about capitals again.

Look at the Raw Daily Data

# Every single daily temperature -----------------------
raw_temp_plot <- dlh_df %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = yearmoda, y = temp)) +

So you cannot see it. That is not a plotting problem — it is a summarizing problem.

Summarize to One Mean per Year

# Mean temperature per year ----------------------------
dlh_year_df <- dlh_df %>%
  group_by(year) %>%
  summarize(temp = mean(temp, na.rm = TRUE))

head(dlh_year_df, 3)
# A tibble: 3 × 2
   year  temp
  <int> <dbl>
1  1948  3.76
2  1949  4.10
3  1950  1.60
  • group_by(year) — one group per year
  • summarize(temp = mean(temp)) — one number per group
  • 28,000 rows become 78
  • na.rm = TRUE ignores days the station missed
Note

Reusing the name temp on the left means every line below keeps working — the same trick from T-Tests II.

Plot the Yearly Means

# Yearly means with the regression line ----------------
yearly_temp_plot <- dlh_year_df %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = year, y = temp)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm")
yearly_temp_plot
  • Now you can see it. The line clearly rises
  • 78 points instead of 28,000 — and the story is clearer, not weaker
  • Still plenty of scatter: single years are weather, the line is climate
  • Exactly the same geom_smooth(method = "lm") as the Regression lecture

But “it looks like it goes up” is not a result. We need the number.

Reading the Warming Rate

Look at the year row of the Coefficients table.

  • Estimate ≈ 0.027 — that is the slope
    • units: °C per year
    • positive → Duluth is warming
    • ×10 → about 0.27 °C per decade
    • over ~78 years → roughly 2 °C
  • Pr(>|t|) is tiny (far below 0.001)
    • the warming is real, not year-to-year luck
  • Multiple R-squared ≈ 0.34
    • year explains about a third of the variation in annual temperature
    • the other two thirds is weather — some years are just cold
    • a modest R² next to a rock-solid p is completely normal in climate data
Important

The intercept looks absurd — about −49 °C.

  • That is the predicted temperature at “year 0”
  • Year 0 is 2,000 years outside the data
  • The intercept is bookkeeping; the slope is the science

A Better Question — Which Season Is Warming?

  • The annual mean says Duluth warmed about 0.27 °C per decade
  • But an annual mean hides which part of the year moved
  • That distinction is the biologically interesting one:
    • ice-out dates and frozen soil depend on winter
    • heat stress and drought depend on summer
    • growing season length depends on the shoulders

The plan — three steps you already know:

  1. mutate() + case_when() to label each day
  2. group_by(year, season) + summarize()
  3. One lm() per season
Important

⚠️ Seasons are not months — they are hemispheres.

  • June–August is summer north of the equator
  • June–August is winter south of it
  • If you pick Melbourne, Santiago or Cape Town later, you must swap the labels
  • A station’s latitude column tells you which hemisphere you are in
    • positive → northern
    • negative → southern

Label Each Day with a Season

# Northern hemisphere seasons --------------------------
# SOUTHERN hemisphere: swap summer and winter
dlh_season_df <- dlh_df %>%
  mutate(season = case_when(
    month %in% c(6, 7, 8) ~ "summer",
    month %in% c(12, 1, 2) ~ "winter",
    TRUE ~ "shoulder"
  ))

# Did it work? -----------------------------------------
dlh_season_df %>% count(season)
     season     n
     <char> <int>
1: shoulder 12718
2:   summer  6436
3:   winter  6287
  • case_when() reads top to bottom: condition ~ answer
    • the first matching line wins
  • month %in% c(6, 7, 8) = “is month 6, 7 or 8?”
  • TRUE ~ "shoulder" is the catch-all for everything left over — spring and fall
  • count() is the check: roughly a quarter of the days in each of summer and winter, half in the shoulder

Summarize by Year and Season

# Drop the shoulder, then one mean per year per season -
season_year_df <- dlh_season_df %>%
  filter(season != "shoulder") %>%
  group_by(year, season) %>%
  summarize(temp = mean(temp, na.rm = TRUE),
            .groups = "drop")

head(season_year_df, 4)
# A tibble: 4 × 3
   year season  temp
  <int> <chr>  <dbl>
1  1948 summer  17.4
2  1948 winter -12.6

  • filter(season != "shoulder") — != means “is not”
    • keeps only the two extremes
  • group_by(year, season) — two grouping columns
    • one row per year per season
    • 78 years × 2 seasons ≈ 156 rows
  • .groups = "drop" tidies up afterwards

This is the same two-column group_by() from T-Tests II.

Plot Both Seasons

# One line per season ----------------------------------
season_temp_plot <- season_year_df %>%
  ggplot(aes(x = year, y = temp, color = season)) +
  geom_point() +
  geom_smooth(method = "lm")
season_temp_plot
  • color = season inside aes() does two jobs at once:
    • colors the points by season
    • makes geom_smooth() fit a separate line per season
  • Two bands, far apart — winter near −11 °C, summer near 18 °C
  • Don’t compare the heights. Compare the steepness.

    • the slope is the warming rate

Winter is warming about 1.6× as fast as summer.

  • This is the expected pattern across the northern continents
  • Fewer extreme-cold nights, shorter ice seasons, earlier thaw
  • The annual mean — 0.27 °C/decade — sat right between the two and told you neither

Reading Two Models Side by Side

Both slopes come straight off the year row of each summary():

Model Slope (°C/yr) ×10 (°C/decade) p
Annual 0.027 0.27 <0.001
Summer 0.021 0.21 <0.001
Winter 0.034 0.34 0.001
  • Winter’s slope has a bigger standard error too — winters are more variable than summers
  • A bigger p with a bigger slope is not a contradiction; it is noisier data

How to write it up:

Mean annual temperature at Duluth rose significantly from 1948 to 2025 (linear regression: slope = 0.027 °C yr⁻¹, equivalent to 0.27 °C per decade; F(1, 68) = 34.3, p < 0.001, R² = 0.34). Winter warmed roughly 1.6 times faster than summer (0.34 vs 0.21 °C per decade).

  • Give the slope with units
  • Convert to per decade so a reader can feel it
  • Report p and R² the same way as any regression

What We Learned Today

The ideas:

  • A regression on time turns a slope into a rate of change — ×10 for per decade
  • Raw daily data hides the trend; group_by(year) %>% summarize() is the fix
  • An annual mean hides which season moved — Duluth’s winter warms ~1.6× faster
  • Seasons are set by hemisphere, not by month number
  • A modest R² with a tiny p is normal — real trend, weather scatter

The code:

  • get_GSOD(years =, station =) and nearest_stations()
  • clean_names() — lower-case names, once, at the top
  • group_by() + summarize() — to the right level
  • case_when() — build the season
  • lm(temp ~ year) + summary() — the rate
