How to turn it in

Run your code in an R script and paste your code and output into a Word document to submit.

ImportantHandwritten sections — read this first

Part 2 and Part 3 below must be answered by hand on paper, photographed clearly, and inserted as images into your Word document. Typed answers to those two parts receive at most half credit, even if the content is correct — I want to see your own sketch and your own reasoning about your own numbers, not a pasted answer. Part 1 (the code) is typed as usual.

Part 1 — Code (typed)

Task 1 — Shape and color together (2 pts)

Make a scatter plot of weight_g (y) vs. height_cm (x), mapping both color and shape to side. Confirm in one sentence whether you get one legend or two, and why.

Task 2 — Add a trend line (2 pts)

Add geom_smooth(method = "lm") to the plot from Task 1 (no color/fill — let it default). Does the trend line slope up or down?

Task 3 — The zoom gotcha (3 pts)

Make two versions of a plot of weight_g vs. height_cm with a geom_smooth(method = "lm") line, zoomed to height_cm between 21 and 23: one using coord_cartesian(), one using xlim(). In 2–3 sentences, explain what’s different about the two trend lines and why.

Task 4 — Theme and save (3 pts)

Pick your favorite built-in theme for a boxplot of weight_g by side, adjust the legend and axis text with theme(), and save the result as both .png (300 dpi) and .pdf to figures/.

Task 5 — Push it further: your own zoom window (4 pts)

This time you choose everything:

  1. Pick your own pair of numeric variables from tree_df that Task 1–3 did not use together (i.e. not weight_g vs. height_cm). You have width_cm available to pair with either one.
  2. Pick your own zoom window — a range on the x-axis that cuts the data roughly in half (look at the range of your x-variable first with summary() or range() so your window is a real, meaningful cut, not a guess).
  3. Build the coord_cartesian() version and the xlim() version of a scatter plot with geom_smooth(method = "lm") for your variable pair and your zoom window, exactly like Task 3 but with your own choices.
  4. Run nrow(tree_df) before zooming, then figure out how many rows the xlim()/ylim() version actually kept. (Hint: filter tree_df to your window by hand with filter() and check nrow() — that’s the row count xlim() is silently using.)

Paste your code, both plots, and state your exact zoom window and the two row counts (before and after) — these numbers will be different for everyone since everyone picks a different window.

Part 2 — Predict, Then Check (handwritten)

Before you run the Task 5 code:

  1. On paper, write down your chosen variable pair and zoom window, and sketch what you predict the coord_cartesian() plot’s trend line will look like compared to the xlim() plot’s trend line — same slope, or different? Steeper or shallower?
  2. Also predict, in a number, roughly how many rows you think will fall inside your zoom window out of the total.
  3. Run the real code.
  4. On paper, underneath your prediction, write 2–3 sentences: how close was your guess on the row count, and did the two trend lines actually differ the way you predicted?

Photograph your prediction and write-up together and insert the photo(s) here.

Part 3 — Explain It, By Hand

Answer by hand, using your own Task 5 numbers (your variable pair, your window, your two row counts) — not generic textbook language:

  1. Using your own before/after row counts, explain in plain language why xlim() produced a different trend line than coord_cartesian() for your specific window. If your two row counts happen to be equal, explain why that could happen and what it tells you about your chosen window.
  2. In your own words — as if explaining to a classmate who missed class — why is it dangerous that xlim()/ylim() delete rows silently, with no warning printed? Give a realistic situation (not necessarily this dataset) where that silent deletion could make someone report a wrong conclusion.
  3. You picked a theme in Task 4. Name it, and explain in one or two sentences why you’d choose it over theme_grey() (the default) for a figure you were putting in a report.
